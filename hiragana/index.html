Two marks that change the sound

Once you know the 46, these come free: the same shapes with a small mark in the top-right corner. Tap to hear.

゛ dakuten — two ticks make it voiced

k → g, s → z, t → d, h → b. Write the two short strokes last.

゜ handakuten — a small circle makes it p

Only on the h row. Draw the circle clockwise, last.

Small ゃ ゅ ょ — two kana, one beat

An i-column kana plus a half-size ya, yu or yo blends into a single sound.

Length matters

A small っ is a beat of silence that doubles the next consonant; a repeated vowel is held twice as long. Both change the meaning.

How it sounds

Japanese has only five vowels, always pure — no sliding into a second sound as English does in "go" or "day". Every kana takes the same short beat.

  • ふfu — blow gently through barely parted lips, like cooling soup. Teeth never touch the lip; it sits between h and f.
  • らりるれろr — a single quick tap of the tongue behind the top teeth, somewhere between r, l and d. Never rolled, never the English r.
  • つtsu — the "ts" at the end of "cats", then a soft u. Start it at the front of the word.
  • し ちshi, chi — not "see" or "tee". In the s and t rows these two break the pattern.
  • うu — lips relaxed and flat, not pushed forward. After k, s, t and h it often whispers away: です sounds like "dess".
  • んn — a full beat of humming on its own. Before b, p or m it turns into m: しんぶん is "shimbun".
  • は へ をparticles — written ha, he, wo but said wa, e, o when they work as grammar words: こんにちは is "konnichiwa".
  • ひhi — a breathy h with the tongue high, close to the German "ich".
